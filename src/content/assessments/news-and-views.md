Journals run a short commentary section on recent papers — *News and Views* at
*Nature*, *Dispatches* at *Current Biology*. A piece runs to about a thousand
words, takes a position rather than summarising, and is written for someone
competent but outside the immediate field.

You are writing one of those, with an investigative constraint: **take a claim
that circulates, find where it came from, and report the difference.**

## The exemplar

You met it in week 2. The mantis shrimp is described almost everywhere as seeing
millions more colours than we do, on the reasonable grounds that it has twelve
photoreceptor types against our three. When Thoen and colleagues tested
wavelength discrimination in 2014, it came out roughly ten times worse than a
goldfish.

The popular claim does not overstate the finding. It inverts it. That is the
shape of thing worth a thousand words.

## Choosing a claim

Good candidates have a specific primary source, a specific circulating version,
and a gap you can characterise precisely. Several are already on the reading
list, which is not a reason to avoid them — arriving at your own account of the
Dunning-Kruger exchange is real work.

What does not work is a claim with no identifiable source, or one where the gap
turns out to be that the popular version is simply less detailed. **Less
detailed is not wrong**, and a piece that spends a thousand words discovering
that journalists simplify has found nothing.

The most interesting outcome available to you is finding that the claim survives
contact with its source. If you investigate honestly and the popular version
holds up, write that. It will mark well, and it is rarer than you would think.

## What to submit

One document. No figure is required; include one if it does work that prose
cannot.

Bring your chosen claim to Rosalind or me by the end of week 5. Most of the
failures on this task are choice failures rather than writing failures, and they
are cheap to prevent and expensive to fix.
