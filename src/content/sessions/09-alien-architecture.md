Most of an octopus's nervous system is not in its brain. The arms carry the
majority of its neurons and retain substantial autonomy, which makes the
vertebrate question — what does the central controller represent — the wrong
first question to ask about it. Godfrey-Smith's *Other Minds* is the reading, and
Hochner's 2012 review is the neurobiology.

This is the week where the course's comparative claim gets its hardest test,
because the octopus is not a differently-sized version of the systems in earlier
weeks. It's a different architecture solving overlapping problems.

## The question, and the objection

Nagel asked in 1974 what it is like to be a bat, and the question has done more
work in philosophy of mind than almost any other four words. You will read it.

You will also read Akins, who argues the question may presuppose something false
— that bat sensory systems are representational in the way the question requires.
If they are not, the question isn't unanswerable so much as malformed, and the
difference matters enormously for what we should conclude from our inability to
answer it.

Regarding which of these is right, I don't have a settled view, and the seminar
is genuinely open. This is one of two weeks where I will argue against my own
lecture.

## A problem nobody has solved

Cephalopods are, as far as we can tell, colourblind. They have one photoreceptor
type. They also produce extraordinarily precise colour-matched camouflage.

Stubbs and Stubbs proposed in 2016 that chromatic aberration plus a strange pupil
shape could recover spectral information from a single channel. It is ingenious
and it is not established. Grade **[S]** leaning **[C]**, and treat it as a
worked example of what a live unsolved problem looks like from the outside —
because most of what you will meet in a career looks like this rather than like a
textbook.
