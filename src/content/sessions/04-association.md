Once a system can link two events so that one predicts the other, it can learn.
It can also be wrong in a new way, and the two facts are the same fact.

Associative learning has access to co-occurrence. It does not have access to
causal structure, and there is no signal available inside the mechanism that
distinguishes a real dependency from a coincidence that happens to be reliable.
Skinner's pigeons in 1948 acquired elaborate stereotyped routines in front of a
food hopper on a fixed timer, and from the pigeon's position the routine was
working.

That is the shape to take forward. The failure is not noise in the system. It is
what the system does when it is running correctly on input that happens to be
misleading.

## Clever Hans

We spend a long hour on a horse.

Pfungst's 1911 investigation of Clever Hans is on the reading list not as a
curiosity but because it established something the rest of the course depends on:
the experimenter is part of the apparatus. The horse was genuinely reading
something. It was reading the questioner, who was genuinely unaware of signalling
anything, and no amount of sincerity on anyone's part prevented the result.

Every method you meet from here on — blinded scoring, preregistration, the
adversarial collaborations in week 12 — is a descendant of that finding. It's also
why Markel's blinding in the pea plant study, from last week, is not a
technicality.

## In the room
