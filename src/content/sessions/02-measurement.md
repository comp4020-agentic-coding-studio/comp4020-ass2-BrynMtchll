The first organism on the course holds nothing. *E. coli* runs and tumbles up a
chemical gradient with a memory measured in seconds, no map, no target, and no
representation of the thing it is swimming towards. It is worth spending an hour
on because almost everything students want to say about it is a projection. The
bacterium does not know where the food is. It knows whether things are getting
better.

That is a measurement plus a comparison, and it is enough to solve a real
problem. Keep it as the floor of the course: whatever we add later, this is what
we are adding to.

## The case that does the work

Then the mantis shrimp, which is the single most useful animal in this course
because the popular story about it is precisely inverted.

You will have heard that it sees millions more colours than we do. It has twelve
photoreceptor types spanning 300 to 720 nanometres, against our three, so the
inference is natural. When Thoen and colleagues actually tested wavelength
discrimination in 2014, it came out roughly **ten times worse** than goldfish,
butterflies, birds or us.

The proposed explanation is that it isn't comparing channels to compute a hue at
all. It reads which of twelve narrow bins fires hardest — recognition rather
than discrimination, closer to a barcode scanner than an eye. Which is a
reasonable thing to build if you strike prey in milliseconds and need to know
*that* rather than *which*.

Hence the lesson that sets up weeks 3 to 7: more sensors is not better sensing.
A sensory system is a commitment about which distinctions are worth the cost of
making, and the mantis shrimp committed to speed.

## In the room

Half the session is a structured argument, in pairs, about whether the 2014
result licenses the sentence "the mantis shrimp has poor colour vision". It does
not, quite, and working out why is the exercise. A 2021 review notes the
mechanism is still unresolved, which is a good early demonstration that **[C]**
is a normal state for a claim rather than a failure of the field.
