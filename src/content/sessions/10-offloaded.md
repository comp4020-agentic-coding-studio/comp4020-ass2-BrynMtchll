The last of the comparative weeks moves the unit of analysis. If a capacity can
sit in a slime trail, it can sit in a group, in a tool, or in a notebook, and the
question of where the cognition *is* stops having an obvious answer.

## The parallel that earns its place

A honeybee swarm choosing a nest site is not loosely like a brain. Seeley and
colleagues showed in 2012 that scouts send inhibitory stop signals specifically
to bees dancing for *other* sites, and that this cross inhibition does the same
job it does between competing neural populations: it breaks deadlock and forces a
decision when evidence is close.

Same circuit motif, same function, thirteen orders of magnitude apart in
substrate. That is the strongest single piece of evidence in the course that
we've been describing a class of solutions rather than a set of animals.

## Where the argument gets contested

Clark and Chalmers argued in 1998 that a notebook you rely on is part of your
cognitive system. Adams and Aizawa's reply — the coupling-constitution objection —
is that coupling a system to a resource does not make the resource part of it,
and that the argument slides between the two. You are expected to be able to
state both.

Then the group-judgement literature, which is a good place to practise holding
two findings at once. Lorenz and colleagues found in 2011 that mild social
influence cut the diversity of estimates without cutting the collective error, so
groups became more confident and no more accurate. Becker, Brackbill and Centola
showed in 2017 that in decentralised network structures, influence *improves*
accuracy.

Both are competent. The disagreement is about network topology rather than about
people, and the seminar is about what that implies for every committee you will
ever sit on.

The rest of the week's reading:

- Clark and Chalmers (1998). [The extended mind](https://doi.org/10.1093/analys/58.1.7). *Analysis*.

- Becker, Brackbill and Centola (2017). [Network dynamics of social influence in the wisdom of crowds](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1615978114). *PNAS* — read these two together or neither.
- Hutchins (1995). *Cognition in the Wild*. MIT Press; Seeley (2010). *Honeybee Democracy*. Princeton University Press.
