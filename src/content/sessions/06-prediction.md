Everything in the course so far has flowed inward: world to sense organ to
representation. This week it reverses. On predictive accounts the system
generates what it expects and passes only the mismatch upward, which means most
of what you experience was manufactured internally and checked against input
rather than delivered by it.

Clark's 2013 target article is the accessible statement and the reading for this
week. Friston's free energy formulation, from 2010, is the ambitious version.

## What it explains, and what that costs you

The reason this framing has taken over is that illusions stop being a list of
curiosities and become predictions. You continue to see the Adelson
checker-shadow wrongly after you have been told exactly how it works, and
knowing does not help, because the inference happens below the level where
knowing operates. Simons and Chabris's gorilla is on the same footing.

Which leads to the sentence that makes this week the one people find hardest: you
do not have access to which parts of your experience were sensed and which were
generated. There's no tag. Both arrive as perception.

## The objection you are required to have

Regarding the free energy principle, you are expected to arrive with a criticism
rather than a summary. The strongest available is the dark room problem — if a
system minimises prediction error, the optimal strategy is a featureless room and
no movement, and the standard reply invoking expected free energy and novelty is
contested. Litwin and Miłkowski's "unification by fiat" is the sharper
methodological version.

Grade the framework <abbr class="grade grade-c" title="contested">[C]</abbr>, and be ready to say what would move it. A theory
that explains everything available is in a slightly awkward position, and saying
why is part of this week's work.

## One distinction to get right

Predictive *coding* is a specific proposal about neural implementation. Predictive
*processing* is a broad framework. The free energy principle is a mathematical
claim of much wider scope. These three get conflated constantly, including in
published work, and keeping them apart is most of what it takes to read this
literature carefully.

## Reading

The paper this seminar turns on:

- Clark (2013). [Whatever next? Predictive brains, situated agents, and the future of cognitive science](https://doi.org/10.1017/s0140525x12000477). *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*.


The rest of the week's reading:

- Friston (2010). [The free-energy principle: a unified brain theory?](https://doi.org/10.1038/nrn2787) *Nature Reviews Neuroscience* — the ambitious version.
- Litwin and Miłkowski (2020). [Unification by fiat: arrested development of predictive processing](https://doi.org/10.1111/cogs.12867). *Cognitive Science* — bring this one to the seminar.
- Simons and Chabris (1999). [Gorillas in our midst](https://doi.org/10.1068/p2952). *Perception*.
- Seth (2021). *Being You*. Faber — the accessible book-length treatment.
