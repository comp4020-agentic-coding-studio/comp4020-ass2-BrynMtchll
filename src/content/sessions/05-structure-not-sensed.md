Tolman argued in 1948 that rats in mazes were not acquiring chains of
associations but something map-like — a representation of layout they could use
in ways their training had never rewarded. O'Keefe and Dostrovsky found cells in
1971 that fired for place. The Nobel followed, eventually, and the story looked
finished: there is a spatial system, here are its cells.

Then in 2016 Constantinescu, O'Reilly and Behrens put people in a scanner and
had them navigate a two-dimensional space of *concepts* rather than places. The
hexagonal grid-like signal turned up anyway, in entorhinal cortex and
ventromedial prefrontal cortex, and it was stable across sessions a week apart.

## Why this changes the shape of the course

If the same machinery organises conceptual relations and physical layout, then
what we have been calling a spatial system is more plausibly a general machine
for relational structure, of which space is one instance — and a conveniently
visible one, which is why we found it there first.

That matters for everything after this week. It means the thing introduced here
isn't a step on the road to cognition. It's closer to the substrate that later
weeks are running on. Whittington and colleagues' Tolman-Eichenbaum Machine, from
2020, is the model that tries to unify the two, and it is on the reading list for
those who want the formal version.

## The limit

A map buys you the ability to act on parts of the world you are not currently
touching. What it costs is that the map can be wrong in ways the map cannot
represent. Spatial judgement distorts in specific, predictable directions, and
the distortion is not available to the person doing the judging — which is the
first appearance in the course of a pattern that will become the whole argument
by week 12.
