Everything here applies to all four assessments, so the individual briefs link
back to this page rather than restating it.

## Late work and extensions

Work submitted without an extension loses 5% of the available marks per working
day or part thereof, and is not accepted after ten working days past the due
date.

Request an extension on or before the due date, by email to the convenor. You do
not need to explain a medical or personal circumstance in detail; a sentence
saying an extension is needed and roughly why is enough. Requests made after the
deadline has passed are harder to grant, not because the rule is punitive but
because the marking is usually underway.

The **week 2 position statement** is the exception. It cannot be extended past
the end of week 3, because its whole function is to record what you thought
before the course changed your mind. A position statement written in week 8 is
not late — it's a different document, and it can't do the job the week 11
revision needs it to do.

## Academic integrity

The usual rule holds: the work you submit is yours, and anything that isn't is
cited. The University's policy governs and this page does not soften it.

One thing is specific to this course. **You are responsible for every evidence
grade you assign.** If you mark a claim as established and it turns out the
literature is split, that is an error in your work, in the same way a
miscalculation would be — not a matter of opinion. This applies however you
arrived at the grade.

## Tools

Use whatever tools you like, including language models, and you do not need to
declare it.

That permission comes with a constraint that is not a formality. The course
assesses whether *you* can tell a supported claim from a satisfying one. A tool
that produces fluent, plausible, uncited prose is very good at generating
exactly the failure this course is about, and it cannot grade evidence for you —
it can only report that something sounds right, which is the thing we spend
twelve weeks learning not to trust.

Two assessments are built with this in mind. The **revision memo** asks what
changed your mind about your own earlier writing, against a document timestamped
in week 2. The **cognitive profile** asks for claims about a real organism that
must survive being checked against the papers you cite. Neither is impossible to
fake. Both are more work to fake than to do.

## Getting help

Ask the person closest to the problem first.

- **Organism choice, the comparative weeks, the profile** — Rosalind Achebe
- **The quizzes, the certainty scale, reading methods sections** — Tuyet Nabarro
- **Assessment, extensions, marks, evidence grades** — Halvard Emms

If that does not resolve it, or if the problem is with one of us, the School
office is the next step, and the University's student assistance services sit
behind that. Nothing in this course is worth a semester of your health.

## If you think the site is wrong

This course publishes evidence grades on contested claims, and some of them will
age badly or are already arguable. If you think one is wrong, tell the convenor.
Corrections get made and attributed, and finding one is a better demonstration of
what the course teaches than any assessment on this page.
