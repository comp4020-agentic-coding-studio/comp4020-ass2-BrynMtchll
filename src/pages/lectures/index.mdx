Mondays, one hour, twelve of them. Every lecture has slides and they are linked
from its page, before the lecture rather than after it — there is nothing in
them worth withholding, and reading ahead makes the Thursday seminar better.

A lecture here is not a summary of the week. It is the place where the evidence
gets put in front of you properly: the actual experiment, the actual numbers,
and the actual disagreement where there is one. That is why several of these
pages name a single paper and spend most of their length on it.

Three of us teach. Halvard takes weeks 1, 11 and 12, which are the weeks where
the course turns on itself. Rosalind takes the comparative weeks — 2, 3, 4, and
then 8 through 10. Tuyet takes 5, 6 and 7, the three weeks where the machinery
gets internal enough to model itself.

Two lectures argue against their own content in the last twenty minutes, and
they are marked as such on the page. That is not a rhetorical device. Week 6
presents predictive processing and then gives you the dark room problem; week 9
presents Nagel and then gives you Akins. If a framework cannot survive the hour
it was introduced in, you should find that out in the room.
