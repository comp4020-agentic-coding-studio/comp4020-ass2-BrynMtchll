Every substantive claim on this site carries one of three grades. You meet the
notation in the [week 1 seminar](/sessions/01-a-world-too-large/), you start
assigning grades yourself in week 3, and by the
[cognitive profile](/assessments/cognitive-profile/) every claim you make has to
carry one. This page is the definition the rest of the site points at.

## The three grades

**[E] — established.** Replicated, or uncontested primary evidence. The claim
has survived contact with people who would have liked to falsify it. A single
well-designed study is not automatically [E]; a single well-designed study that
nobody has successfully contested in twenty years usually is.

**[C] — contested.** There is live disagreement in the field, and the
disagreement is the content rather than an inconvenience. A [C] grade does not
mean "probably false" and it does not mean "we don't know yet". It means that
competent people have looked at the same evidence and reached different
conclusions, and that you are expected to be able to name both positions.
Roughly a third of this course is [C], by design.

**[S] — speculative.** A reasoned extension beyond the evidence, labelled as
such. Speculation is permitted here and it is not a lesser register — the course
ends in it deliberately. What is not permitted is speculation that arrives
wearing the clothes of evidence.

## Why the notation exists

The subject matter makes it necessary. Animal cognition and consciousness
research is unusually full of results that are overclaimed, contested, or have
failed to replicate, and the failure mode for a course with this title is
reciting a satisfying canon that has not held up. Week 11 is largely about how
much of the famous literature on human error did not survive replication.

Hence the rule the whole site follows: **the three registers never blur inside a
paragraph.** If you cannot tell which one you are reading, that is a fault in
the writing, and on this site it is a fault you should report.

## How to assign a grade

Grade the claim, not the paper, and grade it at the resolution the evidence
supports. Most disputes about grades turn out to be disputes about what claim is
actually being made.

The worked example is in week 3. Gagliano and colleagues reported associative
learning in pea plants in 2016; Markel failed to replicate it in 2020 with
blinded scoring; both parties published further and neither conceded. That is
[C]. In the same year Dener, Kacelnik and Shemesh reported risk-sensitive
foraging in pea roots, and it has held up. That is [E]. Two papers, same year,
same plant, same broad claim family, different grades — and being able to say
why is most of what this notation is for.

Conversely, week 9 carries a claim graded **[S] leaning [C]**: the Stubbs and
Stubbs proposal that chromatic aberration plus a peculiar pupil shape could let
a colourblind cephalopod recover spectral information. It is ingenious, it is
not established, and nobody has settled it. Compound grades are allowed when
they are honest. What is not allowed is picking the flattering half.

Note that two of these are graded on a *reading* of a result rather than on the
result itself. The choice blindness data are not in dispute; what is in dispute
is whether they license the strong conclusion, and Petitmengin and colleagues'
2013 work is why. Grading the reading rather than the finding is often the
precise thing to do, and it is worth more marks than grading the finding
bluntly.

## You are accountable for the grades you assign

This is in the [policies](/policies/) and it is worth repeating here. A grade you
assign is a claim you have made. If you mark something established and the
literature turns out to be split, that is an error in your work in the same way
a miscalculation would be — not a difference of opinion, and not excused by how
you arrived at it.

The same standard applies to us. If you think a grade on this site is wrong,
tell Halvard. Corrections get made and attributed, and finding one is a better
demonstration of what this course teaches than anything on the assessment page.
