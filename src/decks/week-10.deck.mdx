# Part 1 — the strongest evidence on this course

---

## The problem a swarm has to solve

A colony divides. Half leaves with the old queen and clusters on a branch.

It now has **days** to choose a new nest cavity, from several candidates, with
no individual able to inspect more than one, and a wrong answer kills the
colony over winter.

---

## How the evidence arrives

Scouts find candidate sites, return, and **dance** for them. Dance vigour scales
with site quality.

Other scouts are recruited, visit, and dance in turn. So support accumulates in
proportion to quality.

That much was known from Lindauer onward, and it looks like simple positive
feedback.

---

## But positive feedback alone deadlocks

Two good sites, each recruiting, each growing. Nothing resolves.

And a swarm that splits between two cavities dies.

So something must break ties. The question is what.

---

## Seeley et al., 2012

Scouts produce **stop signals** — a brief vibrational pulse delivered to a
dancing bee, which causes her to cease dancing.

And the targeting is the finding: a scout preferentially signals bees dancing
for **sites other than her own**.

*Science* 335(6064), 108–111.

---

## Which is a circuit you have already met

![Two panels side by side. Left, a neural decision circuit: two populations accumulate evidence for competing options and mutually inhibit each other until one crosses threshold. Right, a honeybee swarm: scouts dancing for competing sites send stop signals to each other until one reaches quorum. Both are labelled as the same motif with the same function.](./figures/cross-inhibition.svg)

---

## Say what this is and is not

It is **not** "a swarm is a bit like a brain".

It is the same computational motif, mutual inhibition between
evidence-accumulating populations — performing the same function, deadlock
breaking, with the same signature: faster decisions when evidence is lopsided,
slower and more accurate when close.

Thirteen orders of magnitude apart in substrate, with no common ancestor that
had it.

---

## Why that matters for the account

Convergent evolution of a **mechanism**, not a behaviour.

Which is much stronger evidence that we have been describing a class of
solutions to a class of problems, rather than a set of facts about animals.

---

## The follow-up worth knowing

Pais, Hogan, Schlegel, Franks, Leonard & Marshall (2013, *PLOS ONE*) showed the
same cross-inhibition architecture produces **value-sensitive** decision
making: the system commits quickly when options are good, and deliberates when
they are poor.

A property nobody designed in. It falls out of the motif.

# Part 2 — so where is the mind?

---

## Clark & Chalmers, 1998

*The Extended Mind*, *Analysis* 58(1), 7–19.

Otto has Alzheimer's and relies on a notebook. Inga has an ordinary memory.
Both retrieve the museum's address and act on it.

If the functional role is what matters, the notebook is **part of Otto's
cognitive system** — not an input to it.

---

## The parity principle

> If a process in the world functions as a process in the head would, and we
> would call the latter cognitive, then it is cognitive.

Deliberately symmetrical. The burden is on the objector to say why location
should matter.

---

## Adams & Aizawa's objection

The **coupling-constitution fallacy**.

That a system is causally coupled to a resource does not make the resource
*part of* the system. My eye is coupled to a photon; the photon is not part of
my visual system.

Their charge is that the argument slides between coupling and constitution and
treats the slide as an inference.

---

## The reply, and where it stands

Clark's response: the parity principle was never about *any* coupling, but
about a resource that is reliably available, automatically endorsed, and easily
accessible, which the photon is not and the notebook is.

Rupert presses further, arguing the resulting notion of memory is too
heterogeneous to do scientific work.

**[C]**, and you should be able to state both sides. This is one of the two
weeks where philosophy is load-bearing rather than decorative.

---

## And a real case to test it on

Hutchins, *Cognition in the Wild* (1995): navigating a naval vessel.

The fix is computed by a distributed system of people, charts, instruments and
procedures. **No individual on the bridge knows the ship's position.** The
system does.

Ask whether that is a metaphor. Then ask what would settle it.

# Part 3 — and groups fail in their own way

---

## The classic result

Galton, 1907: the median guess of a crowd at a livestock fair beat the experts
at estimating an ox's weight.

Averaging cancels independent errors, so a crowd of noisy estimators can be
more accurate than any of its members.

Note the word doing the work: **independent**.

---

## Lorenz et al., 2011

N = 144. Estimation tasks with real answers. Participants could see previous
estimates — either the average, or the individual values.

Mild social influence was enough to change the outcome.

*PNAS* 108(22), 9020–9025.

---

## What changed, and what didn't

![Two panels. Left, sixty simulated estimators converging over six rounds of social influence, with the group mean staying a fixed distance from the truth. Right, the spread of estimates collapsing from 21 to under 2 while the collective error remains unchanged at 6.7.](./figures/wisdom-of-crowds.svg)

Simulation of the mechanism, not their data.

---

## The mechanism, stated plainly

Averaging is a **variance-reduction** trick. It cancels *independent* error.

Influence removes independence. So the spread collapses, and the shared
component of the error, which averaging never removed, is untouched.

**Spread 21 → 1.6. Error 6.7 → 6.7.**

---

{/* _class: quote */}

The group became far more confident and no more accurate. Confidence is read off disagreement, and disagreement is what vanished.

---

## And a group's blind spot is not the sum of its members'

Each member can be individually well calibrated and the group still wrong, if
the error is shared.

Which is a new failure mode. Nothing in weeks 1 to 9 has this shape.

---

## Becker, Brackbill & Centola, 2017

The rebuttal, and it is a good one.

In **decentralised** networks — no one has a global view, influence is local —
social influence *improves* group accuracy. Theory and experiment.

*PNAS*. So the effect depends on **topology**.

---

## Which is the honest resolution

| Structure | Influence |
| --- | --- |
| Centralised — everyone sees the same aggregate | degrades accuracy |
| Decentralised — local, partial views | improves accuracy |

The literature does not disagree about people. It disagrees about **network
shape**, which is a much more tractable question and a much more useful finding.

---

## Informational cascades

Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer & Welch (1992), *Journal of Political Economy*
100(5), 992–1026.

If each person rationally weighs others' choices, early decisions can lock in a
consensus that nobody's private information supports — and each individual step
is defensible.

Fashion, bank runs, citation patterns, and the reason "everybody knows" is weak
evidence.

# What week 10 established

---

## Four things

1. **Cross inhibition** appears in brains and in swarms, independently, a convergent *mechanism*, which is the strongest comparative evidence available.
2. Whether a notebook is **part of** a cognitive system or coupled to one is unresolved, and the distinction is doing real work.
3. Averaging only helps while errors are **independent**, and influence is what removes independence.
4. A collective can have a blind spot **no member has** — the first genuinely new failure mode since week 3.

---

## Weeks 8 to 10, in one line

Other minds are not a tour. They are the **only vantage point** from which a
bound is visible.

You can state a bee's limits, an octopus's, a swarm's.

Week 11 turns to the one you are inside.
