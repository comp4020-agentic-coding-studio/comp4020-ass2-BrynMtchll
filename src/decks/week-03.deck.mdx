# Part 1 — the slime

---

## *Physarum polycephalum*

A single-celled organism, though one that can cover a square metre.

No neurons. No nervous system. One enormous multinucleate cell, flowing
through a network of tubes it builds and dismantles continuously.

---

## The U-shaped trap

A standard test in robot navigation.

Place a chemoattractant behind a U-shaped barrier. Gradient-following alone
gets you into the U and **cannot get you out** — every local improvement leads
back in.

---

## Reid et al., 2012

*Physarum* solves it. And the mechanism is not internal.

It refuses to cross **its own extracellular slime trail**. Explored ground is
marked as explored, on the ground.

*PNAS* 109(43), 17490–17494. **[E]**

---

## Work the trap

Gradient-following is a rule with no memory:

```
rule:  move toward higher attractant concentration
```

Inside a U-shaped barrier, every direction that raises concentration leads
**further into the U**, because the barrier blocks the short path and the
gradient points through it.

```
step 1:  concentration rising  →  advance
step 2:  concentration rising  →  advance
step 3:  at the barrier, all forward moves blocked
step 4:  retreating lowers concentration  →  rule forbids it
```

**The rule has no exit.** It is not that escape is slow; it is unreachable.

---

## Now add one bit of state

```
rule:  move toward higher concentration, EXCEPT onto your own trail
```

Retreat is no longer forbidden, because the trail marks the path back as
already-explored rather than as lower-value.

**Sanity check:** this is exactly why disguising the trail restores the failure.
Remove the extra bit and you are back to the rule with no exit.

---

## The moral

The organism did not get cleverer. It got **one extra distinction** — visited
against unvisited — and that distinction is stored outside its body.

Which is the cheapest possible upgrade, and it converts an unsolvable problem
into a solvable one.

---

## How they established that it is the trail

Not by observing avoidance, by **removing the cue**.

Disguise the trail and the behaviour breaks. Performance on the U-trap collapses
to gradient-following.

That is the difference between a correlation and a claim.

```notes
Point out the structure: an intervention on the proposed mechanism, not a
richer description of the behaviour.
```

---

## What this architecture buys, and costs

| Property | Consequence |
| --- | --- |
| **Capacity** | effectively unlimited — the substrate is the store |
| **Maintenance** | none — no metabolic cost to holding it |
| **Read speed** | immediate, it is already where it is needed |
| **Vulnerability** | total. Anyone with a pipette can rewrite your memory |
| **Cannot distinguish** | its own trail from another organism's, or fresh from stale |

---

## And there is a follow-up worth knowing

Smith-Ferguson, Reid, Latty & Beekman found the externalised memory helps in
**open or simply-bounded** environments —

and in **complex bounded** environments it confers no advantage and can
actively hinder navigation.

A memory you cannot switch off is not always an asset.

---

## The theoretical suggestion

That externalised spatial memory may be the **functional precursor** to
internalised memory.

Attractive, and speculative. Grade **[S]** — it is a plausible ordering, not
an established lineage.

# Part 2 — the best case study on this course

---

## Jennings, 1906

*Stentor roeseli*, a sessile trumpet-shaped ciliate that builds itself a tube
and feeds by drawing water in.

Jennings subjected it to repeated irritation and described something nobody
expected in a single cell.

---

## An ordered hierarchy of avoidance

![A state diagram. Aversive particles trigger bending away; if the stimulus persists the cell alters its ciliary beat, then contracts into its tube, then finally detaches and swims off. At any stage, if the stimulus stops, the cell returns to normal. The order is fixed and the cost rises.](./figures/stentor-hierarchy.svg)

---

## Why this is a strong claim

Each response is **more costly and less reversible** than the last. Detaching
abandons the tube entirely.

And the order is **fixed**. The cell does not sample randomly from a repertoire;
it escalates.

In one cell. One nucleus. No neurons.

---

## Then it was discredited

During the behaviourist era the result was treated as non-reproducible, and it
stayed that way for most of a century.

Which, on the face of it, is science working correctly. A surprising claim
failed to replicate, so it was set aside.

---

## Dexter, Prabakaran & Gunawardena, 2019

They replicated it. Video microscopy, statistical analysis of response
sequences, and the hierarchy is there.

*Current Biology* 29, 4323–4329.

And they found why the replications had failed.

---

# They used the wrong species

---

## The mismatch

![A timeline: Jennings reports the hierarchy in S. roeseli in 1906; the behaviourist era treats it as non-reproducible; replication attempts use S. coeruleus, which is motile and builds no tube, so faces a different problem; Dexter and colleagues replicate in S. roeseli in 2019. No fraud and no bad statistics — a species name nobody checked.](./figures/stentor-history.svg)

---

## Why the mismatch matters mechanically

*S. coeruleus* is **motile** and builds **no tube**.

An organism that can simply leave has no reason to escalate through
increasingly costly attempts to stay. The hierarchy exists because *roeseli* is
anchored and detaching is expensive.

So the failed replications were not testing a weaker version of the claim. They
were testing a different animal with a different problem.

---

## Sit with this one

No misconduct. No p-hacking. No motivated reasoning.

Everyone in the chain behaved reasonably, and a correct result was thrown away
for **ninety years** because a species name went unchecked.

---

{/* _class: quote */}

This is the failure mode the course is actually about. It is much harder to guard against than dishonesty.

---

## One methodological detail worth having

Dexter and colleagues also found Jennings's original protocol, carmine powder
in pond water — **rarely elicited the behaviour** in their hands.

They had to adjust the stimulation to get it at all.

Which is its own lesson about what "following the published method" secures.

# Part 3 — two plant papers

---

## The same year. The same plant. Different grades.

Both 2016. Both *Pisum sativum*. Both claiming something cognitive.

One has held up. One has not. The seminar is about why.

---

## Gagliano et al., 2016 — associative learning

Pavlovian design adapted for a plant. Pea seedlings in Y-mazes, blue LEDs as
the unconditioned stimulus, a fan as the neutral stimulus.

Three days of training with wind reliably preceding light from one arm.

**Result:** at the choice point, the fan alone influenced growth direction.
Untrained plants were unaffected by it.

*Scientific Reports* 6, 38427.

---

## Markel, 2020 — the replication

Same paradigm. One crucial addition: **blinded scoring.**

Plants were removed from the chamber with fans and lights detached, placed in
random order, and scored by an independent observer while the experimenter was
in another room.

**Result:** no effect. *eLife* 9, e57614.

---

## The exchange, and neither side conceded

**Gagliano et al.** (*eLife* 9, e61141): the protocol was unsuitable. Their own
unconditioned stimulus produced a 100% phototropic response; Markel's produced
only a slight bias, so there was nothing reliable to condition.

**Markel** (*eLife* 9, e61689): the differences cannot account for the failure.
And it is surprising to claim plants *always* grow toward a one-hour light
presentation that followed presentations from the opposite side.

---

## Grade **[C]**

Not "we don't know yet". Not a shrug.

**The disagreement is the content**, and both positions are on the record with
their reasoning. This is what a live methodological dispute looks like from the
outside.

Older attempts at plant conditioning — Holmes & Gruenberg 1965, Haney 1969,
Levy et al. 1970, Armus 1970 — mostly failed to observe it, and those that
claimed it lacked controls.

---

## Dener, Kacelnik & Shemesh, 2016 — risk sensitivity

A split-root design. One root system, two pots.

Both pots have the **same mean** nutrient concentration. One is constant, one
varies over time. The measure is relative root mass in each.

---

## The result, and why it is the interesting one

| Mean nutrient level | Root growth favours |
| --- | --- |
| Low | the **variable** pot — risk-prone |
| High | the **constant** pot — risk-averse |

That is the **energy budget rule** from animal foraging theory. Gamble when
you cannot survive the average; don't when you can.

*Current Biology* 26(13), 1763–1767. **[E]**

---

## Kacelnik's own framing

Not that plants are intelligent.

That an adaptive response to risk **does not require a nervous system**, and
that complex behaviour can be predicted as a biological adaptation without
invoking anything cognitive.

The restraint in that claim is why it survived.

---

## So: why do these two get different grades?

Ask this in the seminar before you answer it.

"One replicated" is the **start** of the answer. The rest involves: which claim
was more surprising relative to prior work, which result depended on
unblinded judgement, and which framing made the smaller demand on the reader.

---

## What week 3 established

1. Memory can live **outside** the organism, and doing so changes what it can and cannot distinguish.
2. A single cell can run an **ordered, escalating** response hierarchy.
3. A correct result can be discarded for ninety years by people behaving reasonably.
4. Two claims about the same organism can deserve different grades, and saying why is a skill.
